Lately I've been spending my weekends reading about corporate tax structures. Not exactly what I pictured myself doing with my free time, but the more I read, the more I realized how little of this I was ever taught.

School taught me how to write a resume and how to interview. It briefly covered how deductions come off your paycheque before you see it. What it didn't cover was how money actually moves once you're past the basics of being a salaried employee, so I started digging into it myself.

## What I'm reading

The list keeps growing:

- CCPCs (Canadian-controlled private corporations) and why they exist
- The small business deduction and what counts as active business income
- Holding structures and how they get used in Canada
- Dividend versus salary as forms of compensation
- The conversations most professionals could be having with their accountants but usually aren't

I've talked to an accountant and I keep a running list of questions in my notes app now, so this has gone a bit past curiosity at this point.

## The gap school doesn't fill

From what I've read so far, there are basically two different tax conversations happening in Canada. One for employees and one for owners.

As an employee, the math is pretty simple, even if the rate is high. You earn a salary, pay tax at your marginal rate, take home what's left and try to save some of it.

As an owner, the math is different. Corporate income can get taxed at a much lower rate when it qualifies for the small business deduction. Money can sit inside the corporation, get invested, or be paid out as salary, dividends or a mix depending on what's tax-efficient that year. Timing, structure and how you pay yourself can change your effective tax rate a lot.

What surprised me is that none of this is some exotic financial trick. It's the default for anyone running a business in Canada. It's in textbooks, CRA publications and all over accountants' websites. You just never come across it if you go to school, graduate and take a salaried job.

I don't think anyone is hiding it on purpose. School is built to produce good employees, and the wealth-building stuff is a different curriculum you have to go find on your own.

## Things I never knew before

A few things that genuinely surprised me as I read:

- The small business tax rate in Ontario is a lot lower than the top personal rate.
- An "active business" has a specific legal meaning, and whether income is active or passive matters a lot for how a corporation gets taxed.
- The order you pay yourself (salary first, then dividends, or the other way around) affects CPP, RRSP contribution room and when the tax gets collected.
- Holding companies aren't just for the wealthy. They're a way to separate operating risk from the money you've built up, and the mechanics are easier to understand than I expected.
- A lot of what gets called "advanced" finance is really intermediate stuff nobody bothered to teach.

To be clear, none of this is financial advice and I'm not an accountant. I'm just someone who never learned this in school and is finding out the basics aren't as out of reach as I thought.

## Why I keep reading

I've spent years getting good at the work side of life. Showing up, doing the work and getting better at the skills I get paid for. That's the employee curriculum, and it's been good to me.

But the more I read, the more I realize that's only half of it. There's a whole other side about how the money you earn gets organized, taxed, invested and held over time, and it's just as learnable as anything else I've taught myself.

I've said this before, but job security is an illusion and skill security is what's real. Financial literacy is one of those skills. The more I understand how money works in Canada, the more options I have.

## A note to myself

You don't have to incorporate, invest or change anything to get something out of this kind of reading. It's about closing the gap, knowing what the words mean and understanding the system you're already in.

I wish I'd started ten years ago, but here I am on a weekend with a coffee, reading about the stuff they never covered in school.
